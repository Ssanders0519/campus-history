# James Fulton Zimmerman and His Impact on the University of New Mexico

James Fulton Zimmerman was one of the most influential presidents in the history of the University of New Mexico (UNM). Serving as the university's seventh president from 1927 until 1944, Zimmerman helped transform UNM from a relatively small educational institution into a growing university with stronger academic programs, research opportunities, and a distinctive campus appearance. His leadership took place during the Great Depression and the beginning of World War II, making his accomplishments especially significant.

James Fulton Zimmerman was born on September 11, 1887, in Glen Allen, Missouri. Before coming to New Mexico, he attended Vanderbilt University, where he earned his undergraduate and master's degrees. He later attended Columbia University in New York, earning his Ph.D. in 1925. His academic background focused on history and political science. In 1925, Zimmerman moved to Albuquerque with his wife, Adella, after accepting a position as a professor of political science at the University of New Mexico. Only two years later, in 1927, he became the university's seventh president. His formal inauguration took place on June 4, 1928, with New Mexico Governor Richard C. Dillon and Stanford University President Ray Lyman Wilbur among those attending.&#x20;

One of Zimmerman's biggest accomplishments was expanding the university's academic programs. In 1928, he established the College of Education, which eventually became today's College of Education and Human Sciences. In 1935, he helped establish the General College, now known as University College. In 1936, he created the College of Fine Arts, providing students with additional opportunities to study creative and artistic subjects. He also supported graduate education, scientific research, and stronger connections with other countries, particularly those in Latin America. These efforts helped UNM become an important educational and research institution in the Southwest.&#x20;

Zimmerman also played an important role in expanding the university's research opportunities. In 1927, he worked with archaeologist Edgar Lee Hewett to establish UNM's Department of Anthropology. The department initially focused on Southwestern archaeology and ethnology, studying the history and cultures of the region. This was important because New Mexico has a long history of Indigenous and Hispanic communities, and the department helped establish a stronger academic interest in those subjects. According to historian D. Fowler's 2003 article, E. L. Hewett, J. F. Zimmerman, and the Beginnings of Anthropology at the University of New Mexico, 1927–1946, Zimmerman and Hewett were both important figures in developing anthropology at UNM.&#x20;

Another major part of Zimmerman's presidency was the physical expansion and architectural development of the campus. He wanted the university to have a consistent appearance that reflected New Mexico's regional history and culture. He supported the Spanish-Pueblo Revival architectural style, which incorporated elements inspired by Pueblo and Spanish Colonial buildings. Zimmerman encouraged Santa Fe architect John Gaw Meem to work with the university and help develop its architectural identity. Some of the most recognizable buildings constructed during this period included Scholes Hall, Anthropology Hall, and Zimmerman Library. The library, completed in 1936, was especially significant because it provided students and faculty with improved research and educational resources. The campus expansion was supported partly by federal New Deal funding during the Great Depression.&#x20;

Zimmerman also strengthened UNM's connections with Latin America. In 1939, he established Latin American Studies, recognizing the importance of New Mexico's geographic, cultural, and historical connections with Latin American countries. In 1941, the School of Inter-American Affairs was established under Professor Joaquín Ortega. These developments helped create a foundation for the university's continuing interest in Latin American research and education.&#x20;

Unfortunately, Zimmerman's presidency ended with his death in 1944. By that time, enrollment at UNM had increased from approximately 610 students in 1925 to nearly 2,600 students. His death occurred during World War II, just before the major increase in college enrollment that followed the war and the passage of the G.I. Bill. His executive assistant, Thomas L. Popejoy, later became president in 1948 and continued the university's expansion.&#x20;

Overall, James Fulton Zimmerman left a lasting impact on the University of New Mexico. His contributions included expanding academic programs, encouraging research, developing anthropology and Latin American studies, and creating the architectural identity that makes the campus recognizable today. His leadership helped establish UNM as an important university in the Southwest. Even after his death, his influence continued through the programs, buildings, and educational opportunities he helped create.
